# Build the walkthrough, not the universe

The idea as pitched is no longer distinctive, but a narrower version is worth building and can run for **$0–11 a month**. Both headline halves shipped elsewhere during 2025–26: Biohub's ESM Atlas maps **6.8 billion** protein sequences with natural-language search, an agent and an MCP server; a free web app has had a language model driving the Mol* viewer since October 2025; and the Human Protein Atlas, cBioPortal, InterPro, ProtVar and Open Targets each added an assistant, AI search or an agent endpoint in 2026, while free skills libraries let any coding agent query the same databases. Reception evidence is discouraging as well: stand-alone protein viewers scored 2–32 points on Hacker News, the nearest comparable explorers have 19 and 49 GitHub stars, and no research thread found demand data from learners or educators. What no existing tool offers is the combination the project's current design already points at: a free, no-login atlas of the **20,429 reviewed human proteins** whose guide emits typed, validated interface commands that are saved as replayable, sourced, editable walkthroughs and exported to the open MolViewSpec scene format. That design is cheap for three reasons: the expensive computation is already published (AlphaFold DB v6 and AlphaMissense under CC BY 4.0, Open Targets under CC0, UniProt's ProtT5 embeddings as a 47 MB download), a replayed walkthrough needs no model call, and a live turn on the cheapest current models costs about **$0.002**. Two 2026 changes widen what is buildable: the ESMC and ESMFold2 models and their official sparse autoencoders are now MIT-licensed, and the Model Context Protocol became stateless, so a public server fits a free edge worker. The same static, login-free architecture satisfies the strictest publication venue, the *Nucleic Acids Research* Web Server Issue. The largest unresolved risk is demand, not feasibility, and it should be tested before the rebuild, not after.

**How to read the evidence.** Unless marked otherwise, a cited statement was read on the linked primary page by a researcher on 25 November 2026. **[C]** marks a fact the research coordinator checked first-hand that day; where research threads disagreed, the [C] fact settles it. The word *reported* marks a vendor's or author's claim about its own work, a secondary source, or a search-result summary. A passage that opens with **Inference** holds reasoning, estimates and recommendations that no source states; it runs to the next heading, and every dollar estimate for compute and model usage sits inside one. Web search ran out part-way through the research (a shared 200-search limit), so later evidence came from direct fetches of known primary pages. Several JavaScript-only sites could not be rendered, and community sentiment on Reddit, Biostars, X and Bluesky is thin.

## Both halves of the pitch shipped elsewhere by late 2026

The original pitch had four parts: a 3D "universe" of real AlphaFold backbones, a molecular structure viewer, a protein–disease–drug knowledge graph, and a language-model guide that reconfigures the interface through typed commands. In November 2026 each part has a better-resourced counterpart. The question for a redesign is therefore not whether the parts can be built, but which combination is still missing and whether anyone wants it.

### Maps exist at 53 million and 6.8 billion proteins, and they draw little attention

Biohub's ESM Atlas, announced on 27 May 2026, organises **6.8 billion sequences and 1.1 billion ESMFold2 structures** by features that sparse autoencoders extract from the ESMC model, not by homology or curated labels ([Biohub](https://biohub.org/?p=42052)). Its data sit in an open S3 bucket under CC BY 4.0, with 7.7 million clusters by feature similarity, and the platform adds natural-language search, an "Atlas agent", an MCP server for outside assistants and a folding app that needs no account ([AWS Open Data registry](https://registry.opendata.aws/biohub-esm-atlas/), [Biohub docs](https://docs.biohub.ai/)). The older Protein Universe Atlas still serves its 2023 similarity network, built from AlphaFold DB v4 models and reported at about 53 million proteins, under CC BY 4.0 ([Expasy](https://www.expasy.org/resources/protein-universe-atlas), [Protein Universe Atlas](https://uniprot3d.org/atlas/AFDB90v4)). At family scale, ProtSpace (MIT, version 4.20.0 released 22 November 2026) explores embedding projections in the browser with annotation overlays, and ProteinCartography builds a structure map around one query protein ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/protspace/), [Arcadia](https://thestacks.org/publications/resource-protein-cartography)).

The reception of these maps is the strongest evidence against leading with one. ProtSpace has **19 GitHub stars** despite active development; ProteinCartography has 49, and its publication is tagged as not actively updating and shows no reader comments ([ProtSpace](https://github.com/tsenoner/protspace), [ProteinCartography](https://github.com/Arcadia-Science/ProteinCartography), [Arcadia](https://thestacks.org/publications/resource-protein-cartography)). The two Hacker News submissions for the ESM launch drew **3 and 2 points and no comments** ([HN search](https://hn.algolia.com/api/v1/search?query=ESM%20protein%20biohub&tags=story&numericFilters=created_at_i%3E1735689600&hitsPerPage=30)). An interactive atlas of 8.5 million research papers did reach 85 points in July 2026, but commenters struggled to name a use beyond its looks, and its creator agreed that the map was the least important of the product's four parts ([HN thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48840207)). ProteinCartography's authors warn that distances in a 2D projection must not be read quantitatively ([Arcadia](https://thestacks.org/publications/resource-protein-cartography)). Sustainability is a recorded failure mode too: after Meta cut the team behind the original ESM Metagenomic Atlas, community groups pinned about 17 TB of it to IPFS to keep it from disappearing (reported) ([LabDAO](https://medium.com/@labdao/how-to-save-20-terabytes-of-scientific-public-goods-23151d24c6b2)).

**Inference.** No existing map combines one organism's whole proteome, continuous zoom from overview to residue, and curated biomedical overlays. That gap is real, but it is a gap in presentation, not in data or method. Competing on scale is pointless: the ESM Atlas holds roughly 28 times as many sequences as AlphaFold DB has predictions. The map should be the way in, not the product. Its layout needs either defensible semantics (labelled neighbourhoods, a similarity network) or a plain statement of what proximity does not mean. A pre-computed static design that costs nothing to keep online answers the sustainability failures directly.

### A model already drives Mol*, and five databases shipped AI surfaces in 2026

The closest prior art to the guide is PlayMolecule AI's "Speak to a Protein", a free web app published in October 2025. It pairs a chat panel with a Mol*-based viewer, and natural-language requests become tool calls that run Python inside the viewer. Its authors report structures that failed to load, calls to functions that do not exist, residue-numbering mismatches between tools, context-window strain and viewer slowdowns, and they evaluated it by case study only ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.17826v1)). ChimeraX gained an `mcp` command with 14 tools in December 2025; the maintainer's page says natural-language control suits newcomers, makes frequent mistakes, forgets them between conversations, and is slower than direct control for experienced users ([UCSF RBVI](https://www.rbvi.ucsf.edu/chimerax/data/claude-dec2025/claude_mcp.html)). Community "talk to PyMOL" bridges are small and often stale: ChatMol has 376 stars and was last pushed in March 2026, and one PyMOL MCP server was created and last touched on the same day ([ChatMol](https://github.com/ChatMol/ChatMol), [pymol-mcp](https://github.com/vrtejus/pymol-mcp)).

The incumbents moved during 2026. Human Protein Atlas version 26.0 (11 November) added AtlasAI, a sidebar of four agents whose documentation stresses that filters are verified before a search runs and that every reported number is checked against the table cell it cites ([HPA releases](https://www.proteinatlas.org/about/releases), [HPA](https://www.proteinatlas.org/learn/ai)). cBioPortalChat (14 August) answers with links back into portal views, runs on Claude Sonnet through Amazon Bedrock, requires Google sign-in and stores conversations indefinitely ([cBioPortal chat docs](https://docs.cbioportal.org/ai-integrations/chat-interface/)). InterPro 110.0 (23 September) shipped an official MCP server with 13 tools and a documentation chat on every page ([EMBL-EBI](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/about/news/updates-from-data-resources/interpro-110-0-and-interproscan-6-a-re-engineered-pipeline-and-ai-ready-access-to-the-protein-universe/)). ProtVar (9 June) added experimental semantic search across more than 500 million possible human missense variants, and its team notes that some users want to browse, not only look up their own variants ([EMBL-EBI](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/about/news/announcements/protvar-update-a-discovery-engine-for-genetic-missense-variation/)). A community registry now lists **77 biomedical MCP servers with about 3,199 tools** ([BioContextAI](https://biocontext.ai/registry)).

Above the databases sits a crowded, well-funded agent layer. Claude Science launched in beta on 30 June 2026 with more than 60 curated skills and connectors, native rendering of 3D protein structures and forkable sessions; it is offered on paid plans and no free tier is mentioned ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-science-ai-workbench)). OpenAI's GPT-Rosalind arrived on 16 April under a trusted-access programme, alongside a free Codex plugin that reaches more than 50 public databases; Google's science skills are open source with 3.5k stars; NVIDIA announced an agent toolkit on 23 June ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-rosalind/), [Google DeepMind](https://github.com/google-deepmind/science-skills), [NVIDIA](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-launches-bionemo-agent-toolkit-giving-ai-agents-the-tools-to-accelerate-scientific-discovery)). An MIT-licensed library of 177 science skills installs into any coding agent and has 58,453 stars ([K-Dense](https://github.com/K-Dense-AI/scientific-agent-skills)).

The reliability record of this layer matters for design. An Anthropic study of 8 June 2026 found agents retrieving viral sequence sets scored **16.9% to 91.3%** mean accuracy without a dedicated tool and varied between identical runs; with a deterministic retrieval tool every agent exceeded 90% ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/agents-in-biology)). Open Targets' own benchmark of 140 question–answer pairs found that generic API wrappers struggle with aggregations, multi-entity queries and interpretation ([Open Targets](https://blog.opentargets.org/official-open-targets-mcp/)). A leading autonomous research system, Kosmos, reports 79.4% of its statements judged accurate, a figure from its developers' own paper ([Kosmos](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02824)).

**Inference.** An assistant on top of a protein database is table stakes, and a deterministic, grounded tool layer under the model is the expected standard, not a differentiator. A 2.0 whose value is "a model that can look things up in protein databases" would compete with free, better-resourced defaults. A small typed command vocabulary is the right engineering response to the published failure modes of free-form viewer control, but users will experience it as reliability, not as a feature.

### Protein viewers score 2–32 points on Hacker News, and experts will not switch

Among Show HN posts mentioning proteins since January 2024, the hits were an open-source AlphaFold 3 implementation (**314 points**), a daily protein-guessing game (88) and the paper atlas (85). A desktop protein viewer scored 32, and the remaining viewers and tools scored between 1 and 11 ([HN search](https://hn.algolia.com/api/v1/search?query=protein&tags=show_hn&numericFilters=created_at_i%3E1704067200&hitsPerPage=50)). Commenters on the desktop viewer asked for plugins, scripting and docking, the features that keep PyMOL dominant, and a structural biologist could not rotate the model ([HN thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44072929)). The game was admired for polish and judged close to impossible for non-specialists ([HN thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46363870)). Star counts tell the same story about developer attention: tens of thousands for agent skills, 5,047 for a generic embedding viewer, and 17 to 376 for protein explorers and model-controlled viewers ([K-Dense](https://github.com/K-Dense-AI/scientific-agent-skills), [Embedding Atlas](https://github.com/apple/embedding-atlas), [ChatMol](https://github.com/ChatMol/ChatMol)). The caveat is that heavily used institutional front ends also have almost no stars, 13 for the Open Targets web app, so stars measure developer attention and not use ([Open Targets web app](https://github.com/opentargets/platform-webapp)).

**Inference.** What travels is something a visitor can use immediately without domain knowledge, or something that confers a capability. Yet another viewer is ignored, and so is anything that requires the visitor to already care about a specific protein. Experts judge viewers by extensibility and workflow fit and will not leave PyMOL or ChimeraX for a web tool; they are realistic as occasional sharers of links, not as primary users. The one niche where practitioners agree an assistant helps is onboarding newcomers.

### Five combinations that no existing tool offers

The gaps are specific. Incumbent assistants return text, tables or links; cBioPortal's documentation says so explicitly ([cBioPortal chat docs](https://docs.cbioportal.org/ai-integrations/chat-interface/)). The story format exists, but each MolViewStories scene is authored in code, and the documentation lists four example stories eighteen months after launch ([MolViewStories](https://molstar.org/mol-view-stories/docs/core-features.html), [examples](https://molstar.org/mol-view-stories/docs/example-stories/index.html)). The Mol* GitHub organisation has no repository for MCP, model or agent integration among 23 inspected, and across all five pages of the 77-server registry the only viewer-control servers target PyMOL and a microscopy viewer ([Mol* repositories](https://github.com/orgs/molstar/repositories), [BioContextAI](https://biocontext.ai/registry?page=2)). Version histories exist as data with no interface found: UniProt keeps 316 dated versions of the p53 entry going back to 1988 ([UniSave](https://rest.uniprot.org/unisave/P04637?format=json)). The strongest agent products are paid, gated or sign-in-only ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-science-ai-workbench), [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-rosalind/)).

The case for a narrower project rests on four observed signals. The incumbents' own teams report that users want to browse and want easier interpretation ([ProtVar](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/about/news/announcements/protvar-update-a-discovery-engine-for-genetic-missense-variation/), [PDBe](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/about/news/updates-from-data-resources/pdbe-entry-pages)). The one protein launch that travelled beyond specialists was playful and visual ([HN thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46363870)). The maintainer of a leading desktop viewer says natural-language control helps newcomers ([UCSF RBVI](https://www.rbvi.ucsf.edu/chimerax/data/claude-dec2025/claude_mcp.html)). And agents become reliable, with differences between models narrowing, once retrieval is deterministic, which is the architecture this project already has ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/agents-in-biology)).

**Inference.** Five combinations were not found in any existing tool: a free, no-login, model-authored and replayable visual walkthrough over real structures and evidence; a single-organism proteome map with continuous zoom to residue and curated overlays; a typed, validated command vocabulary for molecular exploration that is also an open, agent-callable scene format; version-history views across UniProt, AlphaFold DB and interaction evidence; and a protein explorer designed first for learners, with an assistant. Ranked by value, defensibility and feasibility for one developer on at most $20 a month, the first is the product: guided explorations saved as a command log, shareable and forkable, exportable to MolViewSpec so they also play in Mol*. The second is the stage for it: a human-proteome atlas in which every zoom level means something (proteome neighbourhoods, protein, domains, residues and variants). The third makes the vocabulary a reusable component: expose the same commands as agent tools so a visitor's own assistant can drive the atlas. The fourth, a time-machine view of how knowledge about one protein accumulated, is novel and tractable for a bounded set. Evidence-first answers are necessary and no longer distinctive. A layperson-calibrated daily puzzle is a side door for attention. Shared sessions are cheap and have no demand evidence, so they should wait.

The same evidence marks several attractive ideas as already served or likely to fail: a universe-scale map; generic chat with protein databases; an autonomous "AI scientist"; hosted structure prediction or design; replacing desktop viewers for experts; a bigger knowledge graph as the headline; natural-language viewer control as the headline; variant-to-structure lookup for clinicians, which ProtVar, Open Targets and DECIPHER cover with authority; AR/VR, which Mol* already has; and an infinite-canvas research board.

Three risks survive the narrowing. Incumbents are moving toward approachability: ProtVar is repositioning toward browsing, PDBe moved its 3D viewer to the foreground on user feedback, and HPA now generates infographic summaries ([EMBL-EBI](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/about/news/updates-from-data-resources/pdbe-entry-pages), [HPA releases](https://www.proteinatlas.org/about/releases)). A frontier workbench that already forks sessions could add shareable walkthroughs quickly. And the formats this plan would build on have small followings themselves: MolViewSpec has 67 stars and MolViewStories 23 ([MolViewSpec](https://github.com/molstar/mol-view-spec), [MolViewStories](https://github.com/molstar/mol-view-stories)). The four-example count is ambiguous evidence, consistent with high authoring cost and equally with low demand. The absence claims above rest on the pages researchers could open after search ran out; the three most exposed are that no web protein explorer exposes its interface over MCP, that no version-history interface exists, and that no agent layer exists for MolViewSpec. No interview, survey or usage data from students, educators or curious engineers was found. A two-week test, in which three to five hand-authored walkthroughs are shown to a handful of educators, students and practitioners, would buy more certainty than any further desk research.

## The human proteome is 20,429 proteins, and its expensive computation is already published

UniProt release 2026_04, dated 4 November 2026, contains **20,429 reviewed human proteins totalling 11,417,710 residues**. The mean length is 559, the median 415 and the longest 34,350, and 216 proteins exceed 2,700 residues **[C]** ([UniProt release notes](https://ftp.uniprot.org/pub/databases/uniprot/current_release/relnotes.txt)). Every size, cost and rendering budget in this report follows from those numbers, and they are small: the reviewed human proteome is less than 0.01% of AlphaFold DB. That is why one developer can hold all of it in static files while institutes compete on billions of sequences.

### AlphaFold DB, UniProt and Open Targets form a redistributable core

AlphaFold DB v6 was released on 21 October 2025, is synced to UniProt 2025_03 and holds 241,070,489 predictions, including 40,054 isoforms ([EBI release notes](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pdbe/news/alphafold-database-release-notes)). The data are CC BY 4.0 for academic and commercial use ([AlphaFold DB downloads](https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/download)). The human proteome archive, `UP000005640_9606_HUMAN_v6.tar`, is **4.8 GB** **[C]**. The prediction API returns version 6 with BinaryCIF coordinates, confidence, predicted aligned error and a per-protein AlphaMissense file; it returns several entries per accession (eight for BRCA1) and answers HTTP 404 for proteins longer than 2,700 residues, tested on BRCA2 and titin **[C]** ([EBI programmatic access](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/training/online/courses/navigating-alphafold-database/what-is-the-afdb/accessing-searching-afdb/programmatic-access-api/)). Proteins above that length are split into overlapping 1,400-residue fragments that appear only in the human proteome archive ([AlphaFold DB downloads](https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/download)).

UniProt publishes pre-computed ProtT5 embeddings as HDF5 files: one fixed-length vector per protein for Swiss-Prot and for the human reference proteome, with much larger per-residue files hosted separately and sequences longer than 12,000 residues excluded ([UniProt embeddings README, mirror](https://ddbj.nig.ac.jp/public/mirror_database/uniprot/knowledgebase/embeddings/README)). The human per-protein file is **47 MB** **[C]**.

Open Targets Platform release 26.09 (24 September 2026) is marked CC0 1.0 and distributed only as Parquet, from EBI FTP, Google Cloud Storage and BigQuery; quarterly releases are archived from 25.03 onward, each with about 60 datasets **[C]** ([Open Targets licence](https://platform-docs.opentargets.org/licence), [datasets](https://platform-docs.opentargets.org/data-access/datasets)). The release carries 163,181 GWAS Catalog studies, 3.24 million molecular-QTL credible sets, 220,810 clinical reports and gene-constraint metrics updated to gnomAD v4.1.1 ([release notes](https://platform-docs.opentargets.org/release-notes)). STRING 12.5 is CC BY 4.0, and its access page explicitly permits use of its outputs in AI applications such as MCP servers and retrieval pipelines, with attribution ([STRING access](https://string-db.org/cgi/access), [STRING versions page](https://string-db.org/cgi/access?footer_active_subpage=whatsnew)). Reactome version 98 (27 October 2026) applies CC0 to its annotation data ([Reactome](https://reactome.org/pages/about/license-agreement/)). AlphaMissense predictions are CC BY 4.0 and the code Apache 2.0 according to the repository README, while a Zenodo copy still displays an older non-commercial licence **[C]** ([AlphaMissense](https://github.com/google-deepmind/alphamissense), [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/10813168)); this settles a disagreement between research threads in favour of CC BY 4.0. The Human Protein Atlas states CC BY 4.0 for its own copyrightable content, with different terms for some third-party data it redistributes **[C]** ([HPA licence](https://www.proteinatlas.org/about/licence)).

### Three licences decide the dataset's shape

ChEMBL's share-alike term is the main obstacle to a single permissive dataset. Open Targets marks its platform CC0 while its own source table lists ChEMBL and the Human Protein Atlas as CC BY-SA 3.0 **[C]** ([Open Targets licence](https://platform-docs.opentargets.org/licence)); for HPA that table conflicts with HPA's own licence page, and the primary page governs. DisGeNET's non-commercial, share-alike licence rules it out of any open release, and its archived download is still the 2020 version 7.0 ([DisGeNET](https://www.disgenet.org/legal), [downloads](https://www.disgenet.org/downloads)). KEGG prohibits commercial or remotely hosted use, which is why one community MCP server keeps its KEGG tools local-only ([BioContextAI](https://github.com/biocontext-ai/knowledgebase-mcp)). AlphaFold 3 outputs carry CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, as the HPA licence page notes for the AlphaFold 3 structures it hosts ([HPA licence](https://www.proteinatlas.org/about/licence)). Integrated knowledge graphs inherit all of this: Every Cure licenses its own integration work CC BY 4.0 but states that upstream source licences still apply, and PrimeKG's project page states no licence ([Every Cure](https://docs.dev.everycure.org/releases/public_data_releases/), [PrimeKG](https://zitniklab.hms.harvard.edu/projects/PrimeKG)). One optional overlay has unclear terms: Pharos classifies targets into four development levels, about 31% of curated human proteins sat in the least-studied class at its last update paper, and Pharos states no licence of its own, deferring to its primary sources ([Pharos](https://pharos.nih.gov/about), [TCRD paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9825581)).

Several licences were not verified and must be read on the primary page before a derived dataset is published: UniProt's own, ClinVar, gnomAD (a secondary listing conflicts with a researcher's recollection), InterPro, TED, IntAct and BioGRID.

**Inference.** Build the dataset from primary sources with Open Targets as the backbone for disease, genetics and drug evidence, not from an existing integrated graph, because an integrated graph hides the licence audit instead of removing it. Publish it in layers, with a manifest that records source, version, date and licence for every table: a permissive core under CC BY 4.0 (structures, confidence, variant summaries, interactions, pathways, expression), a ChEMBL-derived drug layer under CC BY-SA or fetched at build time, and no DisGeNET or KEGG. Rows inside Open Targets files that originate in ChEMBL deserve the same caution: Open Targets says its sources agreed to unrestricted use by its users, which is not a grant to arbitrary redistributors. The pipeline should read the 4.8 GB archive, not the per-accession API, for three reasons the coordinator's tests exposed: the API returns several entries per accession, it has no models for the 216 longest proteins, and those proteins exist only as fragments, which the interface must label as fragments. AlphaFold DB v6 is synced to UniProt 2025_03 while the protein list comes from 2026_04, so the build should report coverage and sequence mismatches instead of assuming a one-to-one join. A processed, shippable layer plausibly totals 0.3–2 GB, a researcher's estimate and not a measurement. Pin an Open Targets release and test the schema, because 26.09 alone removed or renamed several fields. Key every table on the UniProt accession, with HGNC and Ensembl gene identifiers as join columns and disease identifiers as Open Targets harmonises them; that crosswalk comes from background knowledge and was not checked against sources.

### Download the embeddings, pre-compute the neighbours, rent a GPU once

The largest model-side change of 2026 is licensing. The licence file in the `biohub/esm` repository is MIT, copyright 2026 Chan Zuckerberg Biohub, with no field-of-use restriction **[C]** ([licence](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/biohub/esm/main/LICENSE.md)). The README says it covers ESMC, ESMFold2 and the sparse autoencoders, subject to a separate acceptable-use policy ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/biohub/esm/main/README.md)). The earlier EvolutionaryScale terms pointed the other way, and ESM3-open still sits under a non-commercial licence that bars hosting the model behind an API ([ESM3-open model card](https://huggingface.co/EvolutionaryScale/esm3-sm-open-v1)). The official sparse autoencoders for ESMC-6B expose **16,384 features with only 64 active per residue**, trained at every layer, and emit a sparse tensor of batch by sequence length by codebook size ([SAE model card](https://huggingface.co/biohub/ESMC-6B-sae-k64-codebook16384)). The Hugging Face card carries both an MIT and an "other" licence tag, and says human-readable feature descriptions exist only for the layer-60 model, through the ESM Atlas or the Biohub Platform API **[C]**. ESMC-6B itself has 6 billion parameters and 80 layers and requires a GPU ([ESMC-6B](https://huggingface.co/biohub/ESMC-6B)). Academic precedents exist for the smaller ESM-2: InterPLM publishes interpretable features with an interactive site, and InterProt ships a visualiser built as a React app that calls serverless GPU functions ([InterPLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.12101), [InterProt](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/adams25a.html)).

For embeddings, bigger is not reliably better. In the one direct comparison found, ProtT5 beat ESM-1b and ESM-2 on function prediction, with a molecular-function Fmax of 0.719 against 0.675 ([STAR-GO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.05245)). On a peptide benchmark ESM-2 scored 0.951 at 35 million parameters, 0.958 at 150 million and 0.964 at 15 billion ([pLMFPPred](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.14404)). Those are peptide-level tasks, so they are suggestive, not decisive, for whole-protein similarity.

The supporting tools are mature. Foldseek is GPL-3.0 open-source software, and a published all-against-all run over 542,378 Swiss-Prot structures took 21 hours on 16 threads with its prefilter ([Foldseek](https://github.com/steineggerlab/foldseek), [FoldExplorer](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.18219)). The AlphaFold clusters resource has 2.27 million non-singleton structural clusters, and TED provides about 365 million pre-segmented domains for AlphaFold DB v4 ([AFDB clusters](https://cluster.foldseek.com), [TED](https://zenodo.org/record/13908086)). ProTrek aligns sequence, structure and natural-language function in one embedding space, has open checkpoints at 35 million and 650 million parameters, and by its authors' account beats Foldseek and MMseqs2 at finding functionally related proteins; its licence was not confirmed ([ProTrek](https://zhoubay.github.io/publication/ProTrek), [checkpoint](https://huggingface.co/westlake-repl/ProTrek_35M_UniRef50)). Pre-computed ESM1b variant-effect scores cover all 42,336 human protein isoforms through a public portal ([ESM1b variant portal paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10802723/)). For the browser, ESM-2 exists in ONNX form at 8.1 MB (8 million parameters, 8-bit) and 34.7 MB (35 million), and Transformers.js 4.3.2 runs such models on WebGPU, but no benchmark of protein-model speed in a browser was found ([ESM-2 8M](https://huggingface.co/api/models/Xenova/esm2_t6_8M_UR50D?blobs=true), [ESM-2 35M](https://huggingface.co/api/models/Xenova/esm2_t12_35M_UR50D?blobs=true), [Transformers.js](https://github.com/huggingface/transformers.js/releases)).

**Inference.** Three per-residue tracks are now free or nearly free, and together they are the scientific core of a 2.0: where AlphaFold is confident (pLDDT), where the protein is intolerant to mutation (AlphaMissense), and what a protein language model recognises (sparse-autoencoder features). Each is one array per protein and reuses the per-vertex colouring path the app already has. The layout should be downloaded, not computed. The 47 MB UniProt file is consistent with 1,024-dimension half-precision vectors for about 20,000 proteins, small enough to ship as a static asset; the few proteins over 12,000 residues that UniProt excludes need explicit handling. A second, structure-grounded layout comes from a Foldseek all-against-all run over the human archive. Scaling the published 21-hour run quadratically gives minutes for 20,000 structures, an approximation that needs a real run to confirm. Foldseek's GPL-3.0 licence confines it to the offline pipeline; the neighbour lists it produces are data. Letting a visitor switch between sequence-model space and structure space would make the map more than decoration. TED domains would let the unit of comparison be the domain instead of the whole chain, and Foldseek's 3Di alphabet would let structure be drawn as a 20-letter sequence track beside the amino acids at no 3D cost.

The sparse-autoencoder track is the only capability with a real compute bill. A forward pass of a 6-billion-parameter model over 11.4 million residues is about 1.4 × 10^17 floating-point operations by the 2 × parameters × tokens rule, which is 0.4 to 2 GPU-hours at the 20–100 TFLOP/s effective throughput one researcher assumed; another researcher budgeted $5–50 including failed first attempts. Stored as a feature id plus an activation for 64 features per residue, the raw output is about 2.9 GB, or 0.4–0.7 GB if only the top 8–16 features per residue are kept. None of this is measured: memory needs for ESMC-6B, handling of very long sequences and the size of the autoencoder weights are undocumented in what was read. Two checks come before renting anything. The first is whether Biohub's open bucket already holds per-residue features for the reviewed human proteins, which would turn this track into a download as well; the registry entry describes features for about 6.8 billion proteins, but nobody inspected the granularity. The second is whether the 16,384 layer-60 feature descriptions can be fetched once in bulk. Without labels the overlay shows a feature number, which means nothing to a visitor. If either answer is bad, InterPLM-style autoencoders on ESM-2 deliver the same kind of feature at lower fidelity.

Semantic search ("proteins that pump ions across membranes") is a solved query shape with a dual encoder: embed the text once and take a dot product against 20,429 pre-computed protein vectors. ProTrek 35M is the candidate, pending its licence. A small in-browser ESM-2 is useful only for sequences a visitor types or mutates, where no pre-computed answer can exist; the corpus must then be embedded with the same model, and small-model embeddings are a weak proxy for structural similarity. Both are stretch goals.

### Leave out AlphaFold 3, ESM3-open, AlphaGenome, virtual-cell models and binder design

AlphaFold 3's weights are free only for certain non-commercial uses, are granted at Google's discretion and cannot be redistributed, and its code is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 ([weights terms, mirror](https://code.rc.uab.edu/rc-data-science/community-containers/Alphafold3/-/blob/6166cdc03cb35acb23815aab7aeb749c213c6d8a/WEIGHTS_TERMS_OF_USE.md)). Permissive alternatives at or near its level exist: Boltz-2 (MIT), Protenix-v1 (Apache 2.0), Chai-1 (Apache 2.0), OpenFold3 (Apache 2.0, with a major update and public training data reported in March 2026) and ESMFold2 (MIT, released 27 May 2026) ([Boltz](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jwohlwend/boltz/HEAD/README.md), [Protenix](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bytedance/Protenix/main/README.md), [Chai-1](https://github.com/chaidiscovery/chai-lab), [OpenFold3](https://huggingface.co/OpenFold/OpenFold3/blob/refs%2Fpr%2F12/README.md), [OpenFold3 update](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260313170622/en), [Biohub](https://biohub.org/news/world-model-of-protein-biology/)). Two 2026 caveats apply. Protenix-v2's weights are proprietary according to a repository mirror, so the open status covers version 1 only ([Protenix mirror](https://gitee.com/ByteDance/Protenix)). ESMFold2's claim to beat AlphaFold 3 on interaction benchmarks is unquantified on the announcement pages that could be read ([Rosetta Commons](https://rosettacommons.org/2026/06/09/biohub-releases-open-source-models-that-utilize-evolution-to-design-disease-target-binders/)). A recurring trap is that NVIDIA-hosted versions of permissively licensed models are governed by NVIDIA's own model licences, with the original licence listed only as additional information ([NVIDIA Boltz-2 card](https://build.nvidia.com/mit/boltz2/modelcard)). The CASP16 assessment calls monomer prediction largely solved and complexes unsolved, and found AlphaFold 3 only modestly better than AlphaFold 2 ([CASP16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41178755/)).

On the genome side, AlphaGenome's source code was opened in January 2026 according to press coverage, but its API is free for non-commercial use only, under bespoke terms, and its rate limits are not published ([STAT](https://www.statnews.com/2026/01/28/deepmind-opens-alphagenome-source-code/), [AlphaGenome terms](https://deepmind.google.com/science/alphagenome/terms)). Arc describes Evo 2 as fully open, including weights and training code; its licence file was not read, and the NVIDIA-hosted version again falls under NVIDIA's licence ([Arc Institute](https://arcinstitute.org/news/evo2), [NVIDIA](https://docs.nvidia.com/bionemo-framework/latest/models/evo2/)). Arc's virtual-cell model State ships under a bespoke non-commercial licence and predicts how cell populations respond to perturbation, an output with no natural rendering in a protein viewer ([Arc State](https://arcinstitute.org/tools/state)). The design stack is permissively licensed (ProteinMPNN and LigandMPNN are MIT; RFdiffusion3 is listed as BSD-3-Clause), but BoltzGen's authors recommend generating around 50,000 binders per target ([LigandMPNN](https://github.com/dauparas/LigandMPNN), [Rosetta Commons downloads](https://rosettacommons.org/download/), [BoltzGen](https://jclinic.mit.edu/boltzgen/)).

Free hosted inference is not a foundation either. NVIDIA's own FAQ describes its hosted catalogue as intended for evaluation and prototyping ([NVIDIA NIM FAQ](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/nvidia-nim-faq/300317)). Biohub's ESMFold2 API folds up to 768 tokens synchronously and 4,096 in batch and runs on keys and credits, with no published free quota ([biohub/esm README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/biohub/esm/main/README.md)). ColabFold's public alignment server accepts serial queries from one address and steers bulk users elsewhere ([ColabFold](https://github.com/sokrypton/ColabFold)). Hugging Face ZeroGPU is the one route where visitors spend their own quota: 2 GPU-minutes a day unauthenticated and 5 with a free account, and a free account in good standing can host two such Spaces ([ZeroGPU](https://huggingface.co/docs/hub/spaces-zerogpu)).

**Inference.** Folding is the wrong thing to spend money on: nearly every human protein is already in AlphaFold DB, the 216 longest as fragments, and the app's contribution is interpreting structures, not regenerating them. For a visitor's own sequence, link to Biohub's free folding app in version one and consider a ZeroGPU Space later. Genome and virtual-cell models stay out of a protein-centric explorer; if a "where in the body" dimension is wanted, tissue expression from the Human Protein Atlas delivers it as one more static per-protein value. Design belongs in the app only as explanation. An inverse-folding demonstration on an existing backbone is cheap and legible; a binder campaign at ten designs instead of 50,000 would mislead; and any in-app sequence generation is a dual-use decision that the safety evidence below argues against. Confidence display is itself an honesty feature: CASP16's verdict on complexes is a reason to say on screen that a model is a prediction.

## A typed command log is the agent, the cost model and the product

The app's existing habit of streaming interface commands before prose is the load-bearing idea of the redesign. During 2026 that pattern became standard practice elsewhere, the evidence for grounding it hardened, and the protocol for exposing it to other people's assistants became cheap to host.

### The interface-command pattern became an industry standard in 2026

AG-UI, an open event-based protocol between agents and front ends, is at version 1.0 with streaming chat, shared state, front-end tool calls and human-in-the-loop interrupts; its state events carry whole snapshots or JSON Patch deltas ([AG-UI](https://docs.ag-ui.com/introduction), [AG-UI events](https://docs.ag-ui.com/concepts/events)). Vercel's AI SDK 7, announced on 25 June 2026, supports tools that execute in the client, delivers each call as a typed message part with explicit states, and adds per-tool approvals ([Vercel](https://vercel.com/blog/ai-sdk-7), [AI SDK tool usage](https://ai-sdk.dev/docs/ai-sdk-ui/chatbot-tool-usage)). The best-engineered visual integrations share a recipe. tldraw's agent kit gives the model structured shape data alongside screenshots, defines each capability as a typed action with a schema, repairs common model mistakes such as references to non-existent shapes, and applies an action only once it has finished streaming ([tldraw agent kit](https://tldraw.dev/starter-kits/agent)). OpenAssistant, used with Kepler.gl, splits every tool result into a compact part for the model and a separate payload for rendering ([OpenAssistant](https://github.com/GeoDaCenter/openassistant)). For molecular scenes a declarative target already exists: MolViewSpec describes a view as a tree of typed nodes, with multiple states, animated transitions and per-residue colouring from external annotation files, and Mol* is its reference implementation ([MolViewSpec](https://molstar.org/mol-view-spec-docs/), [annotations](https://molstar.org/mol-view-spec-docs/annotations/)). Fully generated interfaces are a poor fit for core scientific views: Google's own evaluation reports generation times of a minute or more and occasional inaccuracies ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/generative-ui-a-rich-custom-visual-interactive-user-experience-for-any-prompt/)).

**Inference.** Keep a small command vocabulary of the app's own and express it as client-executed tools, so the same commands can be driven by the in-app guide, by tests, by a saved walkthrough and by an outside assistant. Validate and repair every command before applying it. Give the model a compact structured snapshot of view state each turn (selection, filters, camera target, loaded structure, graph root) instead of screenshots. Return a small model-facing result and keep heavy payloads for the renderer. Put every agent action on the same undo stack as user actions. For the structure view the guide should emit MolViewSpec, which is schema-checkable, diffable and serialisable into a link. The decisive consequence is economic: if the guide's output is a typed command log, a finished session is a static file that can be replayed, shared, forked and embedded with no model call. That converts model spend from per-visitor to per-story, which is what makes free use without login compatible with a $20 budget.

### Tools beat memory 0.83 to 0.12, so narration is verified or discarded

Published numbers agree that biomedical answers from model memory are unreliable and that structured tool access closes most of the gap. GeneGPT, which teaches a model to call NCBI's web APIs, averaged **0.83** on a genomics benchmark, against 0.44 for a retrieval-augmented search assistant and **0.12** for ChatGPT alone ([GeneGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09667)). On LABBench2, GPT-5.2 scores 0.058 on database questions without tools and 0.799 on literature questions with them ([Inspect Evals](https://ukgovernmentbeis.github.io/inspect_evals/evals/lab_bench_2/index.html)). Citations written by the model are not a safeguard: SourceCheckup found that 50–90% of responses were not fully supported by the sources they cited, and about 30% of individual statements were unsupported even for GPT-4 with retrieval ([SourceCheckup](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02008)). Free-form query generation has a ceiling as well. On BiomedSQL the best baseline model reaches 58.1% execution accuracy and a custom multi-step agent 62.6%, against 90.0% for domain experts; on the general BIRD benchmark the best system reaches 83.40% against 92.96% for humans ([BiomedSQL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20321), [BIRD](https://bird-bench.github.io/)). Retrieval over model-extracted graphs often does worse than plain retrieval on real-world tasks and helps mainly with complex reasoning and summarisation ([GraphRAG analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.05690)). Scaffolding matters more than specialisation: on end-to-end biomedical machine-learning tasks four agents underperformed human baselines on average, and biomedical specialisation gave no consistent advantage ([BioML-bench](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.09.01.673319v1)).

Provider features now support this style directly. Anthropic's Citations feature extracts the cited text itself, so citations are guaranteed to point into supplied documents, and custom tools can return records as citable search-result blocks; Citations cannot be combined with structured outputs ([Citations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/citations), [search results](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/search-results)). Tool selection degrades beyond 30–50 loaded tools, and deferring tools behind a search tool typically cuts definition tokens by more than 85% ([tool search](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/tool-search-tool)). Cached prompt prefixes are read at a tenth of the input price or less, and any change to tool definitions invalidates the cache ([prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)). Batch processing halves the price of offline work ([batch processing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/batch-processing)).

**Inference.** The architecture that follows is the app's current grounded-first design, generalised to several steps. A deterministic resolver maps names to stable identifiers, and the model never supplies an accession from memory. A handful of typed, parameterised query tools covers the common intents over the local dataset. A read-only SQL tool over the same data serves the long tail, with row limits and returned row identifiers; because free text-to-SQL tops out around 60–85% on public benchmarks, its results must be shown, not merely narrated. Every tool result carries record identifiers and source links. Prose is generated last, with citation markers that code verifies against that turn's tool results; if verification fails, the narration is discarded and the structured answer ships. That turns the 20–30% unsupported-statement rates in the literature into a measured, bounded fallback rate. Graph questions should be answered by deterministic traversals exposed as tools; a model-extracted graph over text would add cost and lose accuracy. Keep five to eight tools always loaded and defer the rest, freeze the system prompt and tool list so the cache survives, and keep bulk data out of the model's context by running queries in the browser and returning only aggregates and identifiers.

### The loop can run in the visitor's browser, on the visitor's key

Where the loop runs is a cost decision. Anthropic's TypeScript SDK lists web browsers as a supported runtime, disabled by default and enabled with the `dangerouslyAllowBrowser` option **[C]** ([anthropic-sdk-typescript](https://github.com/anthropics/anthropic-sdk-typescript)); the underlying opt-in header for direct browser calls dates from August 2024, and bring-your-own-key apps were named as a legitimate use ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2024/Aug/23/anthropic-dangerous-direct-browser-access/)). OpenAI's Node SDK has an equivalent flag ([openai-node](https://github.com/openai/openai-node)). OpenRouter offers an OAuth PKCE flow built for client apps that hands back a user-controlled key with a credit limit and an expiry ([OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/docs/use-cases/oauth-pkce)). Google tells developers never to expose Gemini keys client-side in production ([Google AI Studio](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/api-key)). On the server side, the Claude Agent SDK needs roughly 1 GiB of RAM and a container per agent session, and Pydantic AI (MIT) emits both AG-UI and Vercel AI event streams ([Agent SDK hosting](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-sdk/hosting), [Pydantic AI](https://pydantic.dev/docs/ai/overview/)).

**Inference.** Run the agent loop in TypeScript in the browser or at the edge, with tools executing locally against the in-browser dataset, and keep Python for the offline pipeline and the evaluation harness; an always-on Python service fights the $0 target. The AI SDK is the most complete provider-neutral option for this shape, though a whole-loop-in-browser design is off its documented path and needs a time budget. The Claude Agent SDK is the wrong tool for a browser or edge product. Bring-your-own-key shifts key-theft risk onto the page's defences against script injection: a strict content-security policy, no third-party scripts, the key held in memory by default and never sent to the app's own server, spend-capped keys encouraged, and all fetched literature and database text treated as untrusted input. OpenRouter's own guidance says keys belong in encrypted server-side storage, so a static app that holds a key in the browser is a conscious trade-off, not best practice.

### MCP went stateless in July, so a public server runs on a free Worker

The MCP specification dated 28 July 2026 is the largest revision since launch. It removed protocol-level sessions and the initialisation handshake, added a mandatory discovery call, moved long-running Tasks into an extension, and deprecated Sampling, Roots, Logging and Dynamic Client Registration, with a twelve-month deprecation window ([MCP changelog](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2026-07-28/changelog)). MCP Apps, in which a tool points at an HTML resource that the host renders in a sandboxed iframe, is a stable official extension. The community-maintained client matrix lists support in Claude on the web and desktop, ChatGPT, VS Code Copilot, Microsoft 365 Copilot, Cursor and Goose, and none in Claude Code ([MCP extensions](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/extensions/overview), [client matrix](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/extensions/client-matrix)). The extension's official examples include Three.js scenes and a 3D globe ([MCP Apps](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/extensions/apps/overview)). Authorisation is optional in the specification; Claude accepts authless connectors by default and ChatGPT supports anonymous read-only servers ([MCP authorisation](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2026-07-28/basic/authorization), [Claude connector authentication](https://claude.com/docs/connectors/building/authentication.md), [OpenAI authentication](https://developers.openai.com/apps-sdk/build/auth)). Claude's connector client supports tools, prompts and resources but not sampling or resource subscriptions, and caps a tool result at roughly 150,000 characters ([Claude connector docs](https://claude.com/docs/connectors/building/index.md)). Cloudflare's current guide builds remote servers with a stateless handler, a fresh server object per request and no Durable Object, and publishes an authless template ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/agents/guides/remote-mcp-server/)).

The best design template is the official Open Targets server: five tools, of which two explore the schema in focused subsets, one runs a validated query, one batches queries and one resolves entities ([Open Targets MCP README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/opentargets/platform-mcp/main/README.md)). First-party servers were verified for Open Targets and InterPro. None was verified for UniProt, RCSB PDB, PDBe, AlphaFold DB, Europe PMC or Ensembl, and the popular community per-database servers show no 2026 activity ([Augmented-Nature](https://github.com/Augmented-Nature)). Claude also lets a rendered app view register its own tools, which the model calls to change what is on screen; the feature is limited to 64 tools per view, is web-only and is not enabled for every organisation ([Claude app tools](https://claude.com/docs/connectors/building/mcp-apps/app-tools.md)). The official registry is still labelled preview ([MCP Registry](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/registry/about)).

**Inference.** Ship one stateless, authless, read-only server on the Workers free plan that reads the same pre-built dataset as the site, protected by edge rate limiting and response caching. Design every tool to be useful as text plus structured content, then attach app views (the map, the structure viewer) for hosts that render them; terminal hosts will never show a 3D view. This is the "bring your own assistant" route: the visitor's own model drives the atlas and the owner pays nothing for inference. Do not plan around Sampling as a way to borrow the host's model; it is deprecated, and Claude's connector client does not support it. Serve both protocol eras for a year, expose only sources with open terms, and put licence and attribution in tool output. Sources without a first-party server are better pre-ingested into the app's own dataset than wrapped live. Registry listing is cheap but is not a distribution strategy; host directories are where discovery happens, and they involve review. One open question blocks the richest version: how Mol*, with its large bundle and external structure downloads, behaves under a host's iframe content-security rules is untested.

### A few dozen to 200 tasks, graded by code, make a credible evaluation

Anthropic's January 2026 guide to agent evaluation recommends starting with 20–50 tasks drawn from real failures, combining code-based, model-based and human graders, grading outcomes instead of exact tool paths, and separating capability suites from regression suites that should stay near 100% ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)). Its statistical guidance is to report standard errors and 95% confidence intervals, to cluster errors when questions share context (naive errors were more than three times too small in practice) and to compare systems by paired differences ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/statistical-approach-to-model-evals)). A model used as judge should be built from one expert's binary pass-or-fail critiques and validated by true-positive and true-negative rates on held-out labels ([Hamel Husain](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/)). Inspect, the open-source framework from the UK AI Security Institute, structures an evaluation as dataset, solver and scorer, and its companion collection already implements the main biology benchmarks ([Inspect](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/), [Inspect Evals](https://ukgovernmentbeis.github.io/inspect_evals/)). Public benchmarks are calibration, not a scoreboard, for a project this size: BixBench reported 17% open-answer accuracy for early-2025 frontier models, PubMedQA's leaderboard has been topped by the same entry since November 2023, and FutureHouse argues that a large share of the chemistry and biology answers in one headline benchmark are wrong ([BixBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00096), [PubMedQA](https://pubmedqa.github.io/), [FutureHouse](https://www.futurehouse.org/research-announcements)).

**Inference.** Build the evaluation from the app's own data so that most grading is deterministic. The task families are entity resolution with adversarial cases (ordinary words that collide with gene symbols, unknown names that must be rejected); single-hop lookups with exact answers; multi-hop and aggregation questions answered by reference code; interface-control tasks graded on final state (filters, loaded structure, graph root, camera target); literature questions graded on whether the cited passage contains the claim; out-of-scope and should-refuse prompts; and prompt injection embedded in fetched text. Report task success with n and a 95% interval, all-of-k consistency on a subset, grounded-claim and unsupported-claim rates, the fallback rate, the tool-error rate, cost and latency per task, and refusal correctness, each with the model, date and scaffold version. Run the deterministic subset on every pull request against recorded tool outputs and the full suite nightly at batch prices. Do not claim leaderboard results, accuracy from an uncalibrated judge, or productivity gains. No published protocol for grading agent-driven interface control was found, so the state-diff method is an extrapolation; a small public benchmark of such tasks would be a contribution in its own right.

### Provider biology classifiers refuse benign questions, and bill for it

Current Claude models return a normal response with a refusal stop reason and a category when a safety classifier fires. The documentation for the `bio` category says beneficial life-science work can trigger it, and a `bio` refusal that arrives before any output is still billed ([Claude refusals](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/refusals-and-fallback)). Anthropic's science product says some safeguards are intentionally broad, and the programme that relaxes them is open to organisations, not individuals, and excludes the Fable model family ([Claude Science safeguards](https://claude.com/docs/claude-science/safeguards), [verification programme](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/16975617)). OpenAI likewise accepts that its biology safeguards limit helpful assistance for the general public ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/preparing-for-future-ai-capabilities-in-biology/)). The researchers saw the effect first-hand: the fetch tool's summariser model refused three benign pages on biosecurity and virology-adjacent topics with a biology-classifier reason. That is an observation from this research session, not a published finding.

Usage policies add obligations. Anthropic's policy, effective 12 November 2026, treats diagnosing a medical condition as a high-risk use requiring review by a qualified person, and requires consumer chatbots to disclose that the user is talking to AI ([Anthropic usage policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup)). The protein-design community's 2024 statement commits its 206 signatories to evaluate the risks of design software before release and to order DNA synthesis only from providers that screen ([Responsible AI x Biodesign](https://responsiblebiodesign.ai/)).

**Inference.** Treat a provider refusal as a first-class outcome: detect it, show a neutral explanation, and fall back to the grounded structured answer, which the existing all-or-nothing fallback already provides. Put benign virology- and toxin-adjacent textbook questions in the evaluation set and measure the false-refusal rate per provider. Because visitors bring their own keys, say plainly that the provider's usage policy applies to their requests. Keep the scope clearly on the safe side: human proteins and public reference data, read-only tools, no pathogen genomes, no synthesis or wet-lab protocols, and no server-side execution of model-written code. Position the app as research and education, not for clinical or diagnostic use; disclose AI involvement in every session; and link disease and variant associations to their source and evidence level instead of paraphrasing clinical significance. In-app sequence generation or binder design is the one feature that would move the project into dual-use territory. Visualising and explaining design is the defensible scope for a solo project.

## One instanced draw per level, static files, and a bill under $11 a month

The current renderer draws each protein as its own object, about 108 draw calls for 54 proteins as measured in the repository, and that design cannot reach 20,429. The redesign needs three things from its platform: a renderer that draws the whole proteome in a few calls, a data layer that serves per-protein detail from static files, and hosting whose free limits are understood before launch day.

### WebGPU reaches 83–89% of visitors and 18% on Linux

WebGPU is on by default in Chrome and Edge on Windows, macOS, ChromeOS and Android 12+, in Safari 26 on every Apple platform, and in Firefox on Windows and Apple-Silicon macOS. It is missing or partial on Linux, on Firefox for Android, on Intel Macs in Firefox and on Windows-on-ARM Chrome ([gpuweb wiki](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/wiki/Implementation-Status)). caniuse puts full support at 86.83% of global usage plus 1.8% partial. Web3DSurvey, which counts a working adapter, reports **82.89% overall and 18.03% on Linux** ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/webgpu), [Web3DSurvey](https://web3dsurvey.com/webgpu)).

three.js r187 (30 October 2026) concentrates new work in its WebGPU renderer, which falls back automatically to a WebGL 2 backend, but the manual still calls that renderer experimental ([three.js manual](https://threejs.org/manual/pages/webgpurenderer.html)). A high-priority issue open since February 2025 documents the cost for scenes with many separate objects: 20,000 non-instanced cubes ran at about 60 fps on the classic WebGL renderer and about 15 fps on the new one, and in January 2026 a maintainer measured the new renderer's WebGL 2 backend at about 12 fps against 60 ([three.js issue 30560](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/issues/30560)). Instancing removes the gap: an instanced mesh held 60 fps at 10,000 instances and about 20 fps at 100,000 on classic WebGL, native WebGPU and the WebGL 2 backend alike ([three.js forum benchmark](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/three-js-plant-instancing-benchmark-240-desktop-samples-csv-json-and-a-standalone-harness/95719)). React-three-fiber's stable line is 9.8.2, and its WebGPU-first version 10 is still alpha ([R3F releases](https://github.com/pmndrs/react-three-fiber/releases)). Other production tools are cautious: deck.gl keeps WebGL 2 as its default and calls its WebGPU support not ready for production, and released versions of Mol* render with WebGL ([deck.gl](https://deck.gl/docs/developer-guide/webgpu), [Mol* changelog](https://github.com/molstar/molstar/blob/master/CHANGELOG.md)).

**Inference.** Ship version one on the WebGL 2 feature set and treat WebGPU as a later optimisation. Nothing at 20,429 proteins needs compute shaders, roughly one visitor in six to nine has no WebGPU, and the missing group is concentrated on Linux, where many reviewers and bioinformaticians work. Whether the code targets the classic renderer with GLSL or the new renderer with its shading language and WebGL 2 fallback is a one-day spike, not a research question: the published evidence shows parity for instanced draws and a penalty of roughly two- to five-fold in the reported many-object tests. Either way, pin three.js exactly, stay on react-three-fiber 9, isolate renderer access so the version 10 renames are cheap, and keep the 3D canvas mounted across route changes.

### Four levels of detail fit 11.4 million residues into a WebGL 2 budget

The recipe is proven in molecular graphics. Mol*'s Mesoscale Explorer stores each shape once, draws it with GPU instancing, lowers detail with camera distance and skips what is outside the view or occluded, and it stays interactive on models of up to 3 billion atoms ([Mesoscale Explorer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11409463/)). For points, deck.gl reports about 60 fps up to roughly a million items on a 2015 laptop, and Embedding Atlas renders 4 million points at 60 fps on an M1 Pro, using density contours and map-style labels in place of raw overplotting ([deck.gl performance](https://deck.gl/docs/developer-guide/performance), [Embedding Atlas paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.06386)).

**Inference.** A four-level design fits the evidence. At the first level every protein is a point in a single instanced draw; 20,429 points with 16 bytes of attributes are a third of a megabyte. At the second, the few thousand proteins nearest the camera get a recognisable glyph, either a sprite from a pre-rendered atlas (an 8,192-pixel texture holds 16,384 sprites at 64 pixels, so the proteome needs two) or a very low-resolution ribbon. At the third, the tens to hundreds of proteins that are large on screen get their real alpha-carbon backbone as a camera-facing ribbon, evaluated in the vertex shader from control points in a data texture, with normals pre-computed at build time because per-vertex frames cannot be computed sequentially on the GPU. At the fourth, one protein is handed to Mol* with full atoms. The arithmetic makes level selection mandatory: 11.4 million residues drawn as two triangles each is about 23 million triangles, which a desktop discrete GPU can sustain and an integrated GPU or a phone cannot. Control points stored as 16-bit integers plus one byte of pLDDT come to roughly 80 MB uncompressed for the whole proteome. Culling 20,429 bounding spheres on the CPU each frame is trivial. The layout should be 2D, drawn inside the 3D scene so the camera can tilt and proteins can rise out of the plane on approach; every reference embedding map chose 2D, and cluster labels can be computed at build time and shipped as static JSON. The change that makes all of this feasible is replacing one object per protein with one instanced draw per level. No published benchmark exists for instanced vertex-shader ribbons at this scale, so the budget is extrapolated and needs a prototype.

### Mol*, Nightingale and hyparquet cover structure, tracks and data access

Mol* 5.13.1 (8 October 2026, MIT) is the standard web viewer. The supported integration is the npm package, with either the built-in React interface or a headless plugin context, and the prebuilt viewer bundle is 1.31 MB with Brotli compression ([Mol* plugin docs](https://molstar.org/docs/v5/plugin/instance/), [jsDelivr](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/molstar@5.13.1/build/viewer/molstar.js)). A pLDDT colour theme with the AlphaFold palette is built in ([Mol* source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/molstar/molstar/v5.13.1/src/extensions/model-archive/quality-assessment/color/plddt.ts)). The ChimeraX team discourages that banded palette because the colour break at 70 exaggerates small score differences ([ChimeraX](https://rbvi.ucsf.edu/chimerax/docs/user/alphafold-colors.html)). No reusable component for the predicted-aligned-error heatmap was found. For sequence tracks, EMBL-EBI's Nightingale components are MIT-licensed and regularly released, and `protvista-uniprot` 5.0.0 (23 November 2026) renders domains, sites, variants and an AlphaFold confidence track from one custom element ([Nightingale](https://github.com/ebi-webcomponents/nightingale), [protvista-uniprot](https://github.com/ebi-webcomponents/protvista-uniprot)).

A fully static data layer is realistic. hyparquet (MIT, pure JavaScript, no dependencies) reads Parquet over HTTP range requests ([hyparquet](https://github.com/hyparam/hyparquet)). DuckDB-WASM tracks DuckDB 2.0.1 but costs 6.22 MB of compressed WebAssembly, is single-threaded by default and capped at 4 GB, and its npm `latest` tag is a development build ([DuckDB-WASM docs](https://duckdb.org/docs/2.0/clients/wasm/overview.html), [jsDelivr](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@duckdb/duckdb-wasm@1.33.1-dev57.0/dist/duckdb-eh.wasm), [npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/@duckdb/duckdb-wasm)). Eight-bit quantisation keeps about 97–99% of retrieval quality at a quarter of the storage ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/blog/embedding-quantization)). A researcher's own micro-benchmark, plain single-threaded JavaScript on an Apple M3 Pro, scanned 20,000 vectors of 1,280 eight-bit dimensions in 32 ms. No maintained browser builds of Foldseek, TM-align or Foldcomp were found ([biowasm](https://biowasm.com/cdn/v3/), [Foldcomp](https://github.com/steineggerlab/foldcomp)). Kuzu, the embedded graph database, was archived in October 2025 ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/10/14/kuzudb_abandoned/)). For drawing the knowledge graph, Sigma 4 and cosmos.gl 3 are both MIT-licensed WebGL renderers, while Cosmograph, the product built on cosmos.gl, is non-commercial ([Sigma](https://registry.npmjs.org/sigma), [cosmos.gl](https://openjsf.org/blog/cosmos-gl-v3), [Cosmograph](https://registry.npmjs.org/@cosmograph/cosmograph)).

State, interface kit, accessibility and testing have settled answers. nuqs and TanStack Router both provide typed, validated state in the URL ([nuqs](https://nuqs.dev/docs/options), [TanStack Router](https://tanstack.com/router/latest/docs/framework/react/guide/search-params)). Tailwind CSS is at 4.3, shadcn/ui made Base UI its default primitive layer in July 2026, the Motion animation library is MIT-licensed, and GSAP is free under a proprietary licence that is not open source ([Tailwind](https://tailwindcss.com/blog), [shadcn/ui](https://ui.shadcn.com/docs/changelog), [Motion](https://motion.dev/docs/react), [GSAP licence](https://gsap.com/community/standard-license/)). A canvas exposes nothing to assistive technology, and WCAG 2.2 requires a way to pause motion that starts automatically and lasts more than five seconds ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Reference/Elements/canvas), [W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)). Playwright screenshots vary with host and GPU, and GitHub's GPU runners cost $0.052 a minute and are not free for public repositories ([Playwright](https://playwright.dev/docs/test-snapshots), [GitHub runner pricing](https://docs.github.com/en/billing/reference/actions-runner-pricing)).

**Inference.** Load Mol* from npm in a lazy, client-only chunk, keep one instance mounted for the session and swap structures, and drive it headlessly with controls built in the app's own design system. Keep the AlphaFold palette as the default because visitors recognise it, check its darkest blue against a dark background, and offer a continuous variant. Build the aligned-error heatmap in-house; it is one image with a linked hover. Use Nightingale for the long tail of tracks and draw the three or four signature tracks (confidence profile, domain architecture, variant lollipops, a position-by-substitution heatmap) on a custom canvas. Split the data three ways: a small core table loaded eagerly into typed arrays, per-residue and per-variant Parquet files sorted by accession and read on demand, and DuckDB loaded only when a visitor opens an analytical feature. Ship the k nearest neighbours as a pre-computed column, which removes runtime vector search except for visitor-supplied sequences. Neither a vector database nor a graph database is needed at this scale; by a researcher's arithmetic a compressed-sparse-row graph of even 10 million edges is under about 160 MB in typed arrays. Put shareable view state in the URL (selection, camera pose, colour mode, story step), keep pointer and animation state out of React, and keep pinned items and annotations in an undoable store slice; one view-state schema then serves deep links, story steps, undo and guide commands. Tailwind, shadcn/ui on Base UI and Motion form a current, fully open interface stack. Give every visual view a DOM equivalent, honour reduced-motion preferences by cutting instead of flying, and in CI assert renderer counters along a scripted camera path instead of paying for GPU runners.

### Cloudflare's free limits fit the design, with three traps

Cloudflare is the only vendor surveyed whose free tier combines static hosting, object storage with free egress, a SQL database and a stateful layer. The Workers free plan allows 100,000 requests a day and 10 ms of CPU per request, and returns an error once the daily cap is hit. Static-asset requests are free and unlimited, but a Worker version may hold at most **20,000 static files** of up to 25 MiB each, or 100,000 on the paid plan ([Workers limits](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/platform/limits/)). Static assets ignore Range headers and return the whole file ([static assets](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/static-assets/billing-and-limitations/)). R2 object storage is free up to 10 GB with 10 million read operations a month, then $0.015 per GB-month and $0.36 per million reads, with free egress and Range support; public access should go through a custom domain because the default address is rate-limited ([R2 pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/r2/pricing/), [R2 public buckets](https://developers.cloudflare.com/r2/buckets/public-buckets/)). D1 allows 500 MB per database on the free plan, and SQLite-backed Durable Objects are available free with 100,000 requests a day ([D1 limits](https://developers.cloudflare.com/d1/platform/limits/), [Durable Objects pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/durable-objects/platform/pricing/)). Workers Paid costs $5 a month and includes 10 million requests ([Workers pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/platform/pricing/)). No hard spend cap on the paid plan was found.

Vercel's Hobby plan lists 100 GB of fast data transfer, 1,000,000 function invocations and 4 hours of active CPU a month **[C]** ([Vercel fair use](https://vercel.com/docs/limits/fair-use-guidelines)). Exceeding a limit pauses the feature for up to 30 days, and Hobby projects cannot connect to repositories owned by a GitHub organisation ([Vercel Hobby](https://vercel.com/docs/plans/hobby), [Vercel limits](https://vercel.com/docs/limits)). On frameworks, the OpenNext adapter already treats the app's current Next.js 14 as end-of-life; Next.js shipped a scheduled or emergency security release in every month from July to November 2026; Vite 8 moved to the Rolldown bundler in March; and TanStack Start reached 1.0 on 13 October 2026 ([OpenNext](https://opennext.js.org/cloudflare), [Next.js blog](https://nextjs.org/blog), [Vite 8](https://vite.dev/blog/announcing-vite8), [TanStack](https://tanstack.com/blog)).

For the pipeline, GitHub Actions is free on public repositories, with 4 vCPU, 16 GB of RAM and a 14 GB disk per standard runner and six-hour jobs; scheduled workflows are disabled after 60 days without repository activity ([GitHub billing](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/administering-github-actions/usage-limits-billing-and-administration), [runners](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/runners/github-hosted-runners), [workflow rules](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/how-tos/manage-workflow-runs/disable-and-enable-workflows)). DuckDB 2.0 LTS was released on 21 October 2026 ([DuckDB](https://duckdb.org/release_calendar)). For archival copies, Hugging Face describes public storage on free accounts as best-effort, and Zenodo is reported to allow 50 GB and 100 files per record ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/docs/hub/storage-limits), [Zenodo](https://help.zenodo.org/docs/deposit/manage-files/quota-increase)). Free serverless Postgres tiers cap at 0.5–1 GB per database ([Neon](https://neon.com/pricing), [Supabase](https://supabase.com/pricing)). No surveyed platform offers an always-warm Python container at no cost: Render's free service takes about a minute to wake after 15 idle minutes, and Fly.io's smallest machine costs $2.19 a month ([Render](https://render.com/docs/free), [Fly.io](https://docs.fly.io/about/pricing)).

**Inference.** Host the site as static assets on Cloudflare, with the dataset in R2 behind a custom domain and one small Worker for the model proxy, the MCP server and on-demand entity pages. Three traps follow from the limits. One HTML page per protein already exceeds the 20,000-file cap before any script or stylesheet, so entity pages must be rendered on demand and cached, or the project must take the $5 plan. Anything read by range request must live in R2, not in the asset bundle. And the free daily caps fail closed, so a traffic or abuse spike takes the whole site down until midnight UTC; moving to Workers Paid before a public launch is cheap insurance, paired with a self-built daily budget guard because no platform-side spend cap was found. Concentrating everything in one vendor's account is the residual risk, which is why the data-plane contract should stay file-based (Parquet and binary files on any S3-compatible store), so a self-hosted copy reads identical artefacts.

For the framework, a Vite 8 and React 19 app with a prerender-capable file router (TanStack Start or React Router) has no always-on server runtime and the smallest patching burden. Next.js 16 on Vercel's free plan is the familiar alternative, at the price of pause-on-overage limits and a monthly patch cadence. TanStack Start has been stable for six weeks and coding agents know it less well, so this is a judgment call. Run the pipeline in GitHub Actions with DuckDB writing Parquet shards straight to R2, keep a monthly release so the schedule is not disabled, and publish the canonical versioned dataset on Zenodo for a DOI, with R2 as the serving copy. Use SQL only for small writable data such as saved walkthroughs. Version one needs no user accounts; anonymous sessions and a challenge on the model endpoint are enough. Shared cursors for a few rooms fit the free Durable Objects allowance, about eleven five-person room-hours a day by a researcher's arithmetic, but no demand evidence supports building them first.

### GPU rental is a one-off cost, and a live turn costs $0.002

Rented GPUs are cheap at this scale. Modal lists an L4 at about $0.80 an hour and an A100 40 GB at about $2.10, and its starter plan includes $30 of free credit a month ([Modal](https://modal.com/pricing)). Kaggle's free quota is reported by third parties at about 30 GPU-hours a week ([GPU price summary](https://gpuperhour.com/blog/free-cloud-gpus-and-credits)). Credit programmes are mostly closed to a solo developer without a company or a university: Vercel's open-source programme is not accepting applications, and Google's research credits are reported to go only to faculty, PhD students and postdocs ([Vercel](https://vercel.com/open-source-program), [Google](https://edu.google.com/programs/credits/research/)).

Model prices fell sharply at the low end. Claude Haiku 5.5 and OpenAI's gpt-6-luna both list at **$0.10 per million input tokens, $0.01 for cached reads and $0.50 for output**. Claude Sonnet 5.5 lists at $2, $0.10 and $10, and Claude Fable 5.2 at $8, $0.20 and $40; batch processing halves input and output prices ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing), [OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)). Gemini has a free tier for several models, but free-tier content is used to improve Google's products, and announced price rises take effect on 1 December 2026 and 1 January 2027 ([Gemini pricing](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/pricing)). Chrome's built-in Prompt API is available to web pages from Chrome 148 on desktop with structured output, but it needs 22 GB of free disk, and its documentation does not mention tool calling ([Chrome](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/ai/prompt-api)). For abuse control, Cloudflare's AI Gateway offers caching and rate limiting free, Turnstile is free, and the Workers rate-limiting binding is documented as approximate; the last two facts come from search summaries ([AI Gateway](https://developers.cloudflare.com/ai-gateway/reference/pricing/), [Turnstile](https://developers.cloudflare.com/turnstile/plans), [rate-limit binding](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/runtime-apis/bindings/rate-limit/)). Anthropic offers workspaces and console spend limits ([Anthropic API overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/overview)).

The layering that the numbers support runs from free to paid. Replayed walkthroughs are the default and cost nothing. A visitor's own assistant can drive the atlas through MCP. ## Launch as a login-free web server with a paper trail

The third question is how a rebuilt app becomes both a portfolio centrepiece and a tool people use. The evidence here is the thinnest in the report: named hiring-side sources are few, most career advice is low-quality blogging, and no case was found of a launch directly producing a job offer. The publication venues, by contrast, publish precise rules.

### Interviewers probe judgment, and no source documents a launch that produced a job

One CTO coach's June 2026 account of what he looks for when AI writes the code puts taste and judgment first, followed by ownership, knowing what to ask for, verifying AI output and product sense; his red flags are shipping AI output unread and accepting suggestions reflexively ([AmazingCTO](https://www.amazingcto.com/hiring-developers-when-ai-writes-code/)). A December 2024 survey of 49 hiring managers and engineers found that AI-polished CVs look alike, that recruiters lean on pedigree to cope with volume, and that live discussion of a candidate's deliverable is valued above the code itself ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/how-genai-changes-tech-hiring)). Engineering-leadership outlets report that being unable to explain an AI-assisted solution is a red flag, and that some teams now hand candidates AI-written code to critique ([LeadDev](https://leaddev.com/ai/why-expect-candidates-ai-hiring-process), [Built In](https://builtin.com/articles/audit-interview-scorecard)). A Hacker News moderator is paraphrased as saying that Show HN is flooded with generated repositories and that high-effort projects still get through ([HN thread mirror](https://hn.svelte.dev/item/47213630)).

The case studies found are self-reported, and none involves a protein tool. One developer was hired after about 14 months of contributions to a company's open-source project, and one solo tool drew an investor's enquiry after reaching 400 stars in two days ([DEV](https://dev.to/jwulf/how-to-get-a-job-through-doing-open-source-1a17), [DEV](https://dev.to/piotrkulpinski/growing-a-side-project-to-100k-unique-visitors-in-one-week-29mf)). The best-documented scientific-visualisation example is anywidget: released in February 2023, published in JOSS, and by its authors' count the basis of about 100 widgets by October 2024 ([anywidget](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/10.21105/joss.06939)).

**Inference.** The signals a coding agent cannot fake cheaply are a live deployment that works without login, a written evaluation of the guide with named metrics and failure cases, a decision log that records rejected alternatives, feedback from named practitioners, and depth in one hard problem such as grounded tool-use evaluation or large-scene rendering. Because interviewers probe explanation, the developer should be able to walk through every module without the agent, and should disclose agent use plainly alongside the evidence of verification. Pedigree matters more as application volume rises, so an early-career candidate needs externally verifiable substitutes: a reviewed paper, a named domain advisor, merged pull requests in a known project. Treat "launch leads to job offer" as unproven; the credible path is that visible quality and a publication make interviewers engage. The anti-patterns are undeployed projects, notebook-only AI demos, stacks chosen for keywords, metrics nobody can verify and READMEs that read as generated.

### NAR's rules, the budget and the differentiator point to one architecture

The *Nucleic Acids Research* Web Server Issue is normally published in July. Its current page accepts one-page proposals at any time starting in November 2026 and sets the manuscript deadline at two months after the approval email. Its requirements read like a specification for this project: no login, registration or email address; HTTPS; no tracking or third-party cookies; bookmarkable results; a way to try sample data, with sample output linked from help pages; a standard licence on the landing page; broad interest and simplicity; a scope not confined to a single species unless that species is human or a common model organism; and maintenance for at least five years ([NAR](https://academic.oup.com/nar/pages/submission_webserver)).

JOSS requires a repository that has been public for more than six months with active development, treats commits concentrated just before submission as a warning sign, and requires an AI-usage disclosure confirming that humans made the core design decisions ([JOSS submitting](https://joss.readthedocs.io/en/latest/submitting.html), [JOSS review criteria](https://joss.readthedocs.io/en/latest/review_criteria.html)). ISMB/ECCB 2027 runs from 18 to 22 July 2027 in Copenhagen. Its key-dates page shows no dates yet; the last BOSC cycle had an abstract deadline of 9 April 2026 and required human-written abstracts and disclosed AI use ([ISCB](https://www.iscb.org/ismbeccb2027), [key dates](https://www.iscb.org/ismbeccb2027/key-dates), [BOSC 2026](https://www.open-bio.org/events/bosc-2026/submit/)). *Bioinformatics* accepts application notes of about 2,600 words for novel software, requires web servers to work without login and be supported for three years, and does not accept papers drafted from a prompt ([Bioinformatics](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/pages/author-guidelines)). A Zenodo–GitHub link mints a DOI per release, and a `CITATION.cff` file makes GitHub show a citation prompt ([Zenodo](https://help.zenodo.org/docs/github/describe-software/citation-file/), [GitHub](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/customizing-your-repository/about-citation-files)).

| Venue | Rules that bind this project | Timing | Fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| NAR Web Server Issue ([page](https://academic.oup.com/nar/pages/submission_webserver)) | No login or email; HTTPS; no tracking cookies; bookmarkable results; sample data and help pages; five years of maintenance; six suggested referees | Proposals open from November 2026; manuscript due two months after approval; issue normally in July | Best fit for a login-free web tool |
| BOSC at ISMB/ECCB 2027 ([BOSC 2026 rules](https://www.open-bio.org/events/bosc-2026/submit/)) | Code available under an open licence at review; human-written abstract; AI use disclosed | Conference 18–22 July 2027; 2027 call not found; last deadline was 9 April 2026 | Good first talk or poster |

| JOSS ([criteria](https://joss.readthedocs.io/en/latest/review_criteria.html)) | Public history of more than six months; tests, documentation, OSI licence; research-impact statement; AI-usage disclosure | About six months after 2.0 is public | Later credential |
| *Bioinformatics* application note ([guidelines](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/pages/author-guidelines)) | About 2,600 words; novel software; no login; three years of support; no prompt-drafted text | Any time | Stretch without users |
| Show HN ([rules](https://news.ycombinator.com/showhn.html)) | Runnable without signup; personally built; how and why explained | After a soft launch | Reach, not a credential |

**Inference.** The strictest venue, the budget and the differentiator ask for the same thing. NAR's no-login rule is met by a core tool that works without the guide and a guide that replays stored walkthroughs or runs on a visitor's own key. Its five-year maintenance rule is met by static files that cost nothing to serve. Its bookmarkable-results rule is the URL-addressable view state and the shareable walkthrough link. Its ban on tracking cookies decides the analytics choice. A workable sequence for a relaunch in early 2027 is a NAR proposal once the site is stable, a bioRxiv preprint alongside it, a BOSC or ISMB abstract in early April if the 2027 dates follow last year's pattern, and JOSS about six months after the 2.0 code is public with tagged releases and some outside use. The papers and abstracts must be written by hand, because the venues' AI rules differ and the strictest forbids prompt-drafted text; one AI-usage statement can be written once and reused. A *Bioinformatics* application note is a stretch without users or a novel method. The 2027 deadlines for the Web Server Issue, ISMB and BOSC were not published as dates at the time of research and need checking.

### Show HN rewards a no-signup demo, and r/bioinformatics removes self-promotion

Show HN's rules require something people can run, built personally, easy to try without signup, with an account of how and why it was built. Quickly generated one-off projects and landing pages do not qualify, and a major overhaul of an earlier project can ([Show HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/showhn.html)). Hacker News guidelines prohibit AI-generated or AI-edited comments ([HN guidelines](https://news.ycombinator.com/newsguidelines.html)). A mirrored copy of r/bioinformatics' pinned rules says posts promoting software the poster built are removed; only mirrors were seen ([mirror](https://redlib.cypherpunktools.com/r/bioinformatics)). Self-reported launch write-ups agree on replying to issues the same day and on expecting a first Hacker News post to fail ([HackerNoon](https://hackernoon.com/five-months-of-building-an-open-source-php-dev-tool-what-grew-what-failed-and-what-surprised-me)). Among biologists, the standing complaints about AI are that benchmark scores overstate understanding and, according to one commentary of limited authority, that agents choose the right tools and then draw conclusions the data do not support; EMBL-EBI argues that standardised benchmarks are the basis of reliable claims ([EMBL](https://www.embl.org/news/science-technology/benchmarking-the-foundation-of-reliable-ai-in-biomedicine/), [commentary](https://acribbs.substack.com/p/can-ai-agents-actually-do-bioinformatics)).

**Inference.** Launch in order: a soft launch to two or three named practitioner reviewers, fixes, then Show HN with a working no-login demo, a preloaded example protein and a hand-written paragraph on how agents were used and how their output was verified. In biology communities, ask for feedback instead of posting promotion. Present the project as an exploration and teaching tool, never as discovery. The README should separate what is real (AlphaFold coordinates and confidence values), what is derived (the layout, graph edges, model text) and what is uncertain, with limitations as a first-class section: layout axes are not biological distances, pLDDT is confidence and not accuracy, and model answers can be wrong. Nothing was found on how non-biologist developers earn expert trust, on Biostars or Bluesky norms, on posting times, or on how educators adopt interactive tools; recommendations on those points would be opinion.

### A reusable component earns more attention than another explorer

Developer attention in this field sits with reusable agent tooling, not with stand-alone explorers: the skills library has 58,453 stars and the generic embedding viewer 5,047, against 19 and 49 for the protein explorers ([K-Dense](https://github.com/K-Dense-AI/scientific-agent-skills), [Embedding Atlas](https://github.com/apple/embedding-atlas), [ProtSpace](https://github.com/tsenoner/protspace), [ProteinCartography](https://github.com/Arcadia-Science/ProteinCartography)). anywidget spread as a component that other tools embed, with integrations in Altair and Plotly by its authors' account ([anywidget](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/10.21105/joss.06939)). The Mol* ecosystem already packages its viewer as a notebook widget, ipymolstar, with 55 stars ([Mol* repositories](https://github.com/orgs/molstar/repositories)). Open-source venues set the hygiene floor: an OSI-approved licence, tests and continuous integration, documentation, a contributing guide and tagged releases ([JOSS submitting](https://joss.readthedocs.io/en/latest/submitting.html)).

**Inference.** Publish the command vocabulary as components other people can adopt: the MCP server, a skill or tool that emits MolViewSpec scenes from grounded queries, the versioned dataset with its licence manifest, and the evaluation set with its graders. Each is useful without the app, each sits in the category that currently attracts attention, and contributions to the Mol* ecosystem would be exactly the merged-pull-request signal that substitutes for pedigree. License code under MIT or Apache 2.0 and data under CC BY 4.0, with the share-alike layer separate. Instrument only what can be claimed honestly without third-party cookies: latency percentiles, evaluation scores on the published question set, cost per request, and citations.

## Conclusion

One decision carries the others. The typed command and view-state schema is at once the differentiator (a replayable walkthrough), the cost control (a replay needs no model), the publication requirement (bookmarkable, login-free, maintainable for five years), the evaluation target (interface state can be diffed) and the reusable contribution (agent tools and MolViewSpec export). Designing that schema first, before the renderer or the pipeline, is the highest-leverage week in the project. It also changes what the project is: less an explorer with a chatbot, more an authoring and playback system for sourced molecular explanations, with the atlas as its stage. In a year when a repository and a README can be generated in an afternoon, the scarce signals are provenance and measurement, and the project's existing habit of labelling what is measured, substituted or estimated is the asset to build on.

The remaining uncertainty is ordered and cheap to reduce. Demand comes first: a handful of hand-authored walkthroughs shown to educators, students and practitioners will say more than supply-side gaps can. Four checks follow, each a matter of hours: whether Biohub's open bucket or API yields per-residue features and bulk labels for human proteins; whether an instanced-ribbon prototype holds frame rate on integrated graphics and renders on Linux; what the unverified licences (UniProt, ProTrek, InterPro, TED) actually say; and a hands-on comparison with AtlasAI, the ESM Atlas explorer and PlayMolecule, which this research could not perform. Two findings would change the verdict. If a frontier workbench or the Mol* team ships agent-authored, shareable walkthroughs, the app loses its product claim and should fall back to the component strategy, which remains a credible portfolio piece at near-zero cost. If the demand test finds no audience among learners, the same fallback applies. Time matters at the edges: NAR proposals are open now, Gemini prices rise within weeks, and the app's current framework version is already out of support.
